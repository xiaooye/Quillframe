# Information, uncertainty and revelation

Distinguish what the reader knows, what the viewpoint knows and what remains unknown to both. Choose whether the intended experience is curiosity, suspense, surprise or dramatic irony. These need different information distributions; withholding everything is not a universal solution.

Make an observation available before asking it to support a deduction. Separate the detail from the interpretation when more than one explanation remains plausible. A mistaken theory can be intelligent given the available evidence.

Let a question narrow, branch, become urgent or be replaced. A small answer can reward attention without resolving the whole mystery. When a reveal arrives, let it change a usable judgment, choice or relationship, rather than merely adding another proper noun.

Anchor a reversal in what has been supplied. If the viewpoint cannot know an explanation yet, retain observable consequences and uncertainty. Retrospective clarity should not require inventing an earlier clue.

## Original miniature

The cleaner said the meeting room had been empty since noon. On the polished table, a ring of coffee still overlapped the rectangle where the attendance sheet had lain.

The observation creates a testable contradiction. The narrator has not yet identified a liar or decided when the cup arrived. A thriller may instead disclose the visitor first and build suspense from the cleaner's ignorance.
