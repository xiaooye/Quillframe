# Progression and confrontation

Find what constitutes success in this particular encounter: permission to act, control of a position, a demonstrated skill, protection of someone, or an earned resource. Put that criterion within the reader's reach before the decisive action when the scene benefits from anticipation.

Give the opposition a feasible intention and a way to respond. An exchange becomes interesting when a choice changes the available moves. Follow the relevant physical or social geography so the reader can understand an advantage. Select the decisive observations and judgments; routine exchanges can remain compressed.

Make capability legible through a problem it solves, a risk it recognizes or a limitation it works around. A win need not require an invented injury or sacrifice. Established costs, ongoing obligations and the opponent's response can make success consequential without manufacturing punishment.

Stay with the moment of recognition when it is a promised reward. A witness's reaction earns space when their prior knowledge or stake changes its meaning. Afterward, retain what actually changed rather than resetting the contest with larger numbers.

## Original miniature

Qiao waited until the other boat committed to the narrow bend, then let the current take her stern. The rival had the faster engine; now he had to decide whether to scrape his hull or let her through.

The advantage is understandable because it changes the rival's options. An overwhelming, effortless victory is also legitimate when revealing that imbalance is the scene's purpose.
